# Plain-Language Summary

Colorectal cancer is one of the most common cancers worldwide, and it is highly treatable when caught early. Today's screening tests are either uncomfortable (colonoscopy) or only moderately accurate (the at-home stool tests offered by many clinics). Researchers have wondered whether the trillions of bacteria living in our intestines — measured by sequencing the DNA in a small stool sample — might offer a more comfortable and informative way to screen.

We re-analyzed published data from about 1,500 people across 10 studies in 8 countries to test how well such a test could work. Using a computer model that looks only at which bacterial species are present in a stool sample, we could correctly distinguish people with colorectal cancer from healthy people roughly 78 percent of the time. We also tested whether adding information about what the bacteria are doing chemically would help. Surprisingly, it did not.

The same model could partly distinguish pre-cancerous growths (called adenomas) from full cancer, but could not reliably tell pre-cancer from healthy. We also found and corrected a hidden bias: when training and testing data come from the same country, results look better than they really are. Our reported numbers are honest cross-population estimates.

These results suggest stool-DNA tests are promising but not yet ready to replace existing screening.
